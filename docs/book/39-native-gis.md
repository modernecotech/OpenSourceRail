# Native city editing with RustGIS

A route or station is easier to understand when it can be edited in a
proper map environment. The current development path connects the city
atlas to RustGIS, an editable native project, and back to the service and
cost study. The intended experience is direct: choose a city, download an
editable project with its source tables, make a declared change, return
the saved project and review the revised map and results. The original
published city stays available for comparison.

## What travels with the map

The export includes routes, stations, platforms, original access markers,
interchanges, civil segments, access connectors and unlocated requirements.
It can also carry available roads, buildings, land use, terrain, water,
boundaries and place context, with their source attribution. Tables give
the reader service, energy, cost, workforce and delivery context beside the
geometry. This makes a project useful offline: the editor can see what a
line crosses and which data are genuine source context rather than a new
engineering conclusion.

Some layers are proposals that a planner may edit. Others are calculated
consequences or original sources and must remain read-only. Current admitted
planning edits include route shape, a line's headway or speed cap, station
positions and dwell. A derived civil quantity or price is regenerated by
OpenSourceRail after the return; editing its displayed table is not a way
to approve or import a cost. A change to map colours or camera position
does not create a new railway candidate.

## The return path

After saving the edited native project, the Workbench presents an import
action that asks for an author and a reason. The importer compares the
route and station data against the trusted export. A valid change creates
a planning revision, then passes through the existing bounded city study.
The refreshed map, service, fleet, civil-quantity, price and delivery links
belong to that revision. A reader can keep the parent and child side by
side instead of replacing the source city silently.

The comparison is intentionally narrow. A new station must belong to a
known line and have an explicit dwell. A route or stop with ambiguous
geometry needs correction rather than a silent snap to a convenient
location. Original source identities are protected. Derived layers
remain outputs. A return is refused if it cannot be matched to the
exported city and its applicable model conditions.

## What has actually been demonstrated

In v0.7 development, Samawah and Baghdad have each completed two consecutive
native station edits followed by import, recalculation, refreshed export and
reopening. Those exercises used native project files in automated runs,
without an observed desktop editing session.
They show that the digital round trip can retain source context and produce
a new calculation without redrawing unrelated lines. They are not evidence
of a complete desktop or browser user-experience test. The published
v0.6.1 release did not include a native GIS round trip; its city-atlas
export was held by a mismatch between published GIS and outline sources.

Nor does a successful digital edit establish a real alignment. An unsurveyed
stop can pass interchange checks while being inaccessible across a road.
The map does not reveal all underground utilities, ground behaviour, land
rights or construction space. A planning revision is a proposal until
the city and engineers provide those inputs. RustGIS is useful because it
allows one proposal to be inspected, revised and repriced with its context
intact, not because the software can infer missing field evidence.

## When to return to the planning team

The map is most helpful when disagreements are visible before a design
is frozen. A stop moved to improve access could cross a property boundary,
require a longer elevated approach or worsen interchange with a bus
route. An editor can prepare several revisions and compare their
passenger and cost effects, but the decision should be made with the
city's survey and transport evidence. The revised project should preserve
the reason for every change so a later reviewer can understand why one
alignment was selected.

It is also useful to distinguish a map correction from a design change.
Fixing a mistaken source label should not automatically imply a different
train or civil structure. Altering the line's actual shape may change
length, curves, gradients and foundations, which can invalidate several
downstream studies. A trustworthy workflow reports which dependants were
recalculated and which were carried forward unchanged. That makes native
editing part of the railway's design history instead of an isolated drawing.
