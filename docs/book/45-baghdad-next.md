# Generalising to Baghdad

Samawah is a good first case because its close station markers make a
specific, understandable decision. A platform that works only for this
one edit, however, is not yet a city planning method. Baghdad provides
a second setting with a larger published network and different route
and right-of-way questions. The v0.7 programme uses it to test whether
the same project continuity can survive a different city, not to declare
Baghdad's outline ready for construction.

## Start from the right maturity level

The published v0.6.1 release provides a read-only Baghdad atlas comparison.
Its retained study covers fourteen lines, route and service alternatives,
and provisional costs. The older release did not offer a supported native
GIS export for that atlas, because the GIS and outline publications did
not agree at that source stage. A reader should not infer that a download
button in later development was part of that published feature set.

In current v0.7 development, Baghdad has completed an automated native GIS
return path with two sequential station edits, repricing and a refreshed
project. The result demonstrates digital exchange, preservation of
unrelated line inputs and recalculation under a bounded city workflow.
It does not mean that a Baghdad editor has completed a fully observed
desktop-to-Workbench user journey or that any route has been surveyed.

## What a second city can reveal

The Baghdad case should test conventions that seemed harmless in
Samawah: how a station is tied to its line, how chainage and route shape
are interpreted, how different service policies affect fleet, and how
land or utility allowances are presented. A local price from one city
may not be appropriate in another. A right-of-way estimate can depend
on legal and physical conditions absent from a general map. The second
city should therefore show which assumptions are shared, which are
transferred for planning only and which require new local evidence.

The same candidate discipline applies. The vehicle and propulsion
architecture must be named for each Baghdad comparison. If a different
formation or drive is proposed, its mass, service, civil and cost effects
need their own evaluation. A Baghdad fleet count from a retained atlas
cannot be attached to a Samawah construction estimate merely because
both use six-car trains.

## The reader's comparison

A useful Baghdad journey would let a planner choose one corridor and
make a controlled station or alignment edit. The resulting report would
show the parent and revised maps, passenger access questions, line
service, fleet, civil quantities, energy and all fourteen cost scopes.
Unchanged lines would remain visible as unchanged. The project view
would explain any refused edit, ambiguous station placement or missing
site cost. It would also show that the cost and delivery drafts belong
to the edited Baghdad case rather than to a generic city default.

The purpose of the second city is confidence in method. It can expose
assumptions that a first demonstration overlooked, while retaining the
important boundary between a functioning planning workflow and a
buildable railway. A surveyed alignment, demand study, local rates,
ground and utilities, station access and candidate-specific operation
will still be needed before a Baghdad investment or engineering choice.

## Compare without transferring false certainty

Suppose the Baghdad edit changes a station location but leaves the line
length almost constant. The fleet could remain unchanged while walking
access, land and station costs move substantially. Another edit could
alter a line's geometry and trigger civil quantities and travel-time
changes even with the same stations. The reports should make these
different dependency patterns easy to see. A shared vehicle design may
be reusable, while its city-specific power, structure and service
calculations may not be.

The second city should also help improve the questions asked of the first.
If Baghdad reveals that a transferred utility rate is especially
uncertain, Samawah's estimate may deserve the same scrutiny. If the
native editor exposes an ambiguity at an interchange, the city model
should record it rather than forcing an arbitrary resolution. Reuse
and generalisation are valuable when they uncover such differences,
not when they make two unlike places appear identical.
