# Stopping, faults and rescue

The safest journey is one whose unusual moments have been designed as
carefully as its normal motion. A train can lose a launch, stop between
stations, encounter poor adhesion, meet an occupied route or need to be
evacuated in heat. A proposed drive and timetable are credible only when
the train can be brought to safety and the passengers cared for in these
states.

## Braking belongs to the train

Station-side equipment may help accelerate or receive a train, but the
reference requires service, emergency and parking braking independent of
that equipment. This gives the train a means to stop after a station power
fault or missed receiving event. It does not remove the need to prove the
brake's actual force and repeated thermal capacity. Wheel and rail adhesion
varies with water, contamination and load; wheel-slide protection has to
preserve steering and stopping rather than assume a fixed friction number.

Stopping distance includes perception or detection, control response,
equipment build-up and actual deceleration. It depends on initial speed,
gradient, mass, available adhesion and the faults considered. One retained
downhill, low-adhesion calculation from 20 metres per second gives a
distance around 700 metres when response is included. That is a warning
about the size of the problem, not a validated minimum separation. The
protected route and service headway must ultimately use the accepted
worst relevant cases and approved margin.

## A failed drive changes more than arrival time

If a station cannot launch a train, passengers may be held at a platform.
If reception fails, the train needs a safe independent stopping location.
If it stops between stations, the project must decide how it will be
secured, communicated with, reached and moved. Gradients, curves, weather
and the absence of a continuous powered rail can make recovery difficult.
A candidate may need a rescue vehicle, a tow interface, local storage or
another provision. Whichever path is chosen has mass, civil access,
maintenance and operating costs.

Emergency planning also changes stations and guideways. An elevated track
must have a way for responders to reach people and for people to leave a
disabled train, including those with reduced mobility. The guideway needs
working space and protection from adjacent traffic or power equipment.
Communications, lighting, ventilation and battery endurance must last
long enough for the chosen procedure. These are candidate requirements,
not generic promises from a diagram of a train at a station.

## Faults can combine

A hot day can increase cooling demand at the same time that a power system
is stressed. A vehicle fault can lengthen dwell and disrupt the following
train. A crowded carriage can change brake load during poor adhesion.
Engineering should therefore test combinations that matter, while keeping
the scenarios bounded and explainable. It is equally important to show
what has *not* been tested. An apparently safe result under normal dry
conditions cannot be borrowed for a wet, loaded, downhill fault.

The current platform has software controls and conditional journey models
that can exercise parts of these scenarios. Actual propulsion availability,
brake behaviour, thermal endurance and human evacuation need measurements,
procedures and independent review. Recording that boundary is a practical
part of safety: it tells the next engineer what evidence is still needed
before a timetable or design can be accepted.
