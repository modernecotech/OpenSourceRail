# From a city brief to an alignment

A city does not need an identical railway merely because another city has a
promising train. Population, topography, heat, flood risk, streets, utility
networks, land rights, transfers and local production capacity change the
answer. The planning tools begin with geography and a transport need, then
make each route and station proposal explicit. The objective is a comparison
that a city can question and revise, rather than a map whose apparent precision
hides missing surveys.

## Start with people and places

An initial brief describes where people travel, when they travel, what
connections they need and which journeys are currently difficult. It should
include walking and wheelchair access, climate, travel time, comfort, safety
and an affordability range. Many of these inputs are incomplete early on. A
useful outline can still be made if the missing demand, prices or site facts
are labelled as assumptions with a clear plan to improve them.

The city catalogue brings together route lines, stop and transfer markers,
street and place context, and other available geographic layers. A planner can
propose an alignment, move a stop, alter a dwell assumption or change the
service. The revised proposal is then evaluated with a declared vehicle and
propulsion policy. Its fleet, energy, civil quantities, cost and dossier can be
compared with the parent proposal. An edit to one station need not recalculate
an unchanged carbody; a change in a common material price may affect many cost
accounts while leaving the geography alone.

| Early question | Evidence needed before a build decision |
|---|---|
| Where should the line go? | Surveyed terrain, land, utilities, structures and local access |
| Where should a station sit? | Walking routes, platform fit, transfers, foundations and evacuation |
| How often should trains run? | Demand, dwell, protected separation, terminals and spare fleet |
| What does it cost? | Candidate quantities, local rates, site works, risk and renewals |

## Maps are a starting point

A visible route is not yet a buildable alignment. Railway horizontal and
vertical geometry, gradients, drainage, bridge interfaces, foundations,
access roads and temporary construction space have to be checked. A station
marker may be close to a desired destination but difficult to reach across a
major road. A neat viaduct line may intersect a utility or require costly
ground treatment. The Workbench can identify such questions and show which
results depend on them; it cannot create a survey that has not happened.

Native GIS editing is being connected to this process. An editor can export a
city project, change a station and return the edit for recalculation. The
Samawah and Baghdad development journeys have exercised two consecutive
native edits each, including repricing and a refreshed project. Those tests
show that the software can carry an edit through a defined workflow. The
survey accuracy and engineering acceptability of the route remain separate.

## Keep the proposal attached to its assumptions

Each corridor revision should retain the route geometry, stops, chosen train,
service assumptions and price basis that produced its result. This prevents a
later design from borrowing an attractive cost or journey time from a different
candidate. It also lets a reader see which inputs changed when two outcomes
differ. If the edit reduces one platform or changes one dwell, the report should
show the effect on station count, travel time, trainsets, energy, civil scope,
capital cost and whole-life cost. A zero change is informative when it is
verified; it should not be silently omitted.

The best first result is therefore a *questionable, traceable outline*. It
should be detailed enough to expose the major trade-offs, honest about missing
site evidence, and easy to revise when a city supplies better information.
