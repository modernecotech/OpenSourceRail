# A corridor cost comparison

Costs become more useful when tied to a service a city could recognize.
OpenSourceRail retains a thirty-kilometre elevated, double-track comparison
with twelve stations. It assumes a 72 km/h speed limit, one metre per second
squared acceleration, a two-minute peak interval and 276 named places per
reference train. The calculation uses 52 six-car trains, including its
fleet and spare assumptions. This is a design-stage benchmark, not a
priced order or a decision to build that particular corridor.

## Two prices can describe different assumptions

One generic-rate Class C central estimate is about US$738 million, or
US$24.6 million per route-kilometre. It includes trainsets, stations,
civil works, a provisional compact launch and power concept, depot,
production and tooling, testing and contingency under its declared rates.
A separate project-volume supply comparison reprices the same corridor
and fleet using wholesale and manufacturer observations and calculated
manufacturing alternatives. Its central installed estimate is about
US$548.1 million, or US$18.27 million per route-kilometre. Shared premises,
freight, quality work, duties and future replacement setup are counted in
that comparison.

The difference is not a verified saving available to every city. The two
studies test price and supply assumptions, while the physical design and
service control remain provisional. Neither has supplier quotations for a
complete qualified railway or a surveyed site. A local owner would need to
replace transferred prices, ground assumptions, access, land and utilities,
construction method, tax and financing terms with its own evidence.

| What is held in the benchmark | What a new city must establish |
|---|---|
| Thirty-kilometre, twelve-station study scope | Actual route, access, land and ground |
| Reference train, places and two-minute control | Demand, operating plan and protected headway |
| Provisional drive and civil families | Candidate-specific equipment and structure |
| Scenario rates and model quantities | Supplier bids, local labour and site quantities |

## Use a benchmark to ask better questions

If civil work dominates the price, a city may investigate span form, station
placement, construction access and utility diversions. If rolling stock
or propulsion dominates, it can compare formations and drive families at
equal passenger service. If the fleet is large because round-trip time is
long, a station or route change might have more value than a small saving
on each car. The point of a model is to direct evidence-gathering and
design effort toward the choices that matter most.

The Samawah case illustrates a different scale of question. Its midpoint
station edit barely changes the modelled capital total yet increases the
whole-life present cost through energy. That is not contradictory. Capital,
operating cost and passenger usefulness respond to different parts of the
design. A lower first cost does not guarantee a better whole-life choice.

Affordability should be measured alongside journey quality, availability,
accessibility and local support. Mandatory safety and passenger protection
are constraints on every candidate. The benchmark's figures make the
discussion concrete; they become investment evidence only when the selected
physical candidate, service, site, suppliers and risks have been reviewed
together.
