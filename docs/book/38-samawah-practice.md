# Samawah before and after

The Samawah station example is small enough for a reader to follow and
large enough to reveal the system's dependencies. Two source markers on
one line sit about eighty metres apart. The planning case assumes a
120-metre platform, so treating them as separate full stations would need
an explanation. The proposed edit replaces the two markers with one at
their midpoint. It does not move the route itself.

## Read the starting case

In the development Workbench, the reader opens Samawah in the city atlas
and chooses the station-merge comparison. Before looking at the cost
delta, it helps to note the scope: the five-line network, the selected
vehicle reference, the explicit service assumptions and the fact that
the source markers had already been grouped into one operational complex.
The network is a planning model. Its stations, ground and walking access
have not been fully surveyed and qualified.

The unedited case contains 26 raw markers and 25 operational station
complexes. The edit leaves 25 raw markers and still 25 operational
complexes. The modelled route remains about 57.87 kilometres and the
fleet remains 81 trainsets. Those equalities are meaningful. The edit
cleans up a problematic pair of markers but does not, under the current
rules, remove a built operational station or one train from the fleet.

## Follow the changed accounts

The base installed capital estimate falls by about ten thousand dollars.
The base whole-life present cost rises by about two hundred and thirty
thousand dollars. Line 1's one-way travel time grows by roughly one and
a half seconds; the other four lines do not change. The energy account
is the main contributor to the higher whole-life result. An access-repair
allowance is replaced by a shorter proposed access length with its own
risk provision. The report shows all fourteen cost scopes, including
those with no change, so the reader can separate the consequences of
this edit from the size of the entire city programme.

| Question | What the comparison says | What it cannot decide |
|---|---|---|
| Did a station disappear? | One raw marker did; operational complexes stayed at 25 | Whether the real station should be at either marker or the midpoint |
| Did the fleet shrink? | The model still needs 81 trainsets | The final timetable, spare policy or protected headway |
| Did construction become cheaper? | The provisional capital model falls slightly | Site ground, utilities, access and supplier prices |
| Is the revised journey better? | One line is a little slower in the model | Actual walking routes and passenger preference |

## Do not lose the difficult findings

The comparison retains a line-1 coast-reach question and the absence of
accepted propulsion and braking force evidence. It continues to display
transferred rates, unknown local works, platform fit, catchment and
walking-access questions. The existing civil quantity identity and the
vehicle/manufacturing reference are reused because this marker edit did
not change their defined inputs. That is an engineering statement about
the model's scope, not a guarantee that a surveyed site would leave them
unchanged.

A retained compact-drive study considered a similar midpoint idea using a
different body candidate. Its results remain a research comparison. They
are not inserted into this passive-wheel case merely because the city
name and station pair match. If a planner wants to compare the drive
alternative, they need its own complete vehicle, service, civil and
economic result.

The next decision belongs on the ground. A midpoint might shorten the
distance between original markers but put an entrance on the wrong side
of a road, a utility corridor or an inaccessible parcel. A useful follow-up
would map real pedestrian approaches, potential platform edges, land and
ground constraints, then run the chosen location through the same
comparison. The software makes the trade-off inspectable; it does not
choose a station for the people of Samawah by arithmetic alone.

## How to use the case in a public discussion

The comparison can support a focused conversation with residents and
planners. People can mark the walking routes they actually use, the
crossings they avoid, the destinations reached by each original marker
and the time of day when access matters most. The city can add land,
utility and flood information. A station designer can test platform
orientation, lift locations and emergency exits. Each new input can
become an explicit proposal for another study rather than an informal
annotation left outside the cost calculation.

The result should be presented as a range and a set of trade-offs. The
model's ten-thousand-dollar capital difference is small beside the
roughly billion-dollar provisional programme total and could be overtaken
by one site-specific item. The whole-life change is similarly conditional
on energy and service assumptions. The important outcome is the
traceable direction of those accounts, not a claim of precision to the
last dollar. A city decision may favour accessibility or reduced disruption
even when one modelled cost account moves the other way.
