# Baghdad and retained comparisons

Baghdad gives the published platform a second, very different city to
inspect. Its retained atlas packet includes fourteen lines and a calculated
route and service comparison. A reader can move from the map to line
assumptions, fleet and cost, then ask which findings might survive a local
survey. In v0.6.1 this is a **read-only example**. It should not be described
as a new calculation performed during the Samawah demonstration.

## A larger city changes the questions

A fourteen-line network raises different issues from one corridor. Transfers
may matter as much as the running time on a single line. A station placement
can change access for one neighbourhood while affecting several journeys
through the network. The service plan has to coordinate line frequencies,
train availability, interchanges and terminal capacity. A useful comparison
therefore asks not only how much guideway is drawn, but what passenger
journeys the network offers.

The retained atlas can display proposed routes and their service and cost
sensitivities. It can show where a line's length, station pattern or fleet
assumption influences the result. It cannot establish actual demand, land
take, right of way, utilities, ground or construction disruption. A generic
allowance for such work is a way to keep a missing account visible, not
evidence that the work has been surveyed and priced.

## A retained result has a particular value

The Baghdad packet is useful because it preserves a complete, inspectable
design-stage calculation. A reader can see the assumptions that made it
and compare them with Samawah or with another proposal. But its source
identity and publication conditions belong to that retained study. Reading
it today does not update its geography, software method or rates. If the
city provides a more recent survey or changes its planning policy, a new
evaluation should be made with those inputs.

This distinction is especially important when a platform carries many
historical studies. A number on an old chart may be numerically exact for
its old model and still be irrelevant to a current investment question.
Keeping the source and evaluation attached lets the reader use historical
work as context, while preventing it from becoming unearned current proof.

## The native-map boundary in the published release

The v0.6.1 atlas does not offer a supported editable RustGIS project export
for Baghdad. The GIS and outline publications at that release do not have
matching source epochs, so the platform holds the native export. The
read-only map and source-bound result remain available. Later v0.7 work
implements and tests an edit, return and reprice path under new source
conditions; those later results are discussed separately.

A city team can still use the published Baghdad study to plan evidence
collection. Which station catchments deserve field walking surveys? Which
right-of-way or utility corridors are uncertain? What service frequency is
worth testing against observed demand? Which quantity accounts need local
rates or supplier input? Each answer should lead to a clearly named new
proposal and evaluation, rather than an invisible alteration to the
retained packet.

Baghdad thus shows both the strength and the limit of an open city atlas.
The strength is that many connected questions can be inspected together.
The limit is that a map and model cannot substitute for the local knowledge,
survey, public engagement and physical engineering needed to choose and
build a corridor.

## Compare two cities without copying one answer

Samawah and Baghdad can help test whether a planning method generalizes.
The same questions about passengers, stations, fleet and cost can be asked
in both places. The answers should not be imported across cities. The
larger Baghdad network, its land and utility situation, and its actual
travel patterns need their own inputs. A useful comparison might ask
whether a similar headway produces a very different fleet requirement,
or whether station access dominates in one corridor while construction
conditions dominate in another. A model that explains such differences
is more useful than one that merely prints comparable totals.

For a public discussion, the retained Baghdad map should be presented
alongside a short list of what people can challenge. Are the destinations
right? Are interchanges accessible and safe? Would the route divide a
neighbourhood? Which assumptions reflect local observation, and which
were brought from a generic model? The answers can form the brief for a
new, locally supported study. The retained atlas is the starting page
of that conversation, not its conclusion.
