# How to read a city route map

The city maps in this part are invitations to ask transport questions. A
coloured line shows a proposed route through available geographic context.
Station symbols show operational platform groups, and interchange references
show intended connections between lines. Water, streets, land use and place
names help a reader understand why a route might serve one corridor and miss
another. None of these marks means the alignment has been surveyed or that a
station entrance can be built where its symbol sits.

## What a map can already tell us

A network outline makes several questions visible at once. Is the proposal a
few long radial corridors, a dense cross-city web, or a web with a ring? Where
would passengers change lines? Do the longest journeys pass through several
interchanges, and could a shorter local journey be made without crossing the
city centre? A map can reveal a possible service gap or an unexpectedly close
pair of station markers. Samawah's proposed merge began with precisely that
kind of observation.

The legend deserves attention. Different colours identify proposed lines,
while small symbols distinguish an operational stop from an original access
marker or an interchange reference. Several symbols can refer to one
possible station complex. A line shown near a named place indicates map
proximity, not a verified entrance there. The scale bar is useful for
orientation, but an accessible walking route should be measured along
actual streets, crossings and level changes.

The catalogue also attaches modelled service and cost records to each city.
Route length, number of lines, operational platform groups and fleet size can
be read together with the map. In the examples that follow, fleet figures
include a modelled reserve and assume one declared six-car reference and
service policy. They are neither an order for trains nor a prediction of
actual demand. The broad catalogue gives a common starting point for
comparing cities while leaving site-specific decisions to each city.

## What the symbols do not establish

A short straight line between a station and a street can be a schematic
access connector. It is not a measured walking route, step-free entrance or
legal right of way. An operational platform group may combine several raw
source markers. It is not necessarily one built platform or one land parcel.
An interchange reference says where two services might connect; transfer
distance, accessible circulation, fire escape and timetable coordination
remain to be designed.

The background geography has its own limitations. An open map may be very
helpful for locating rivers or broad street patterns, yet omit a utility,
recent building, retaining wall or land restriction. Digital screen
distances are not construction chainages. Curves, grades, drainage, bridge
clearances, foundations, temporary works and the space needed for a station
can change the route materially. This is why a planning outline should lead
to survey and local engagement before an attractive line hardens into an
engineering promise.

## Compare cities without equating them

The ten examples range from a five-line Samawah outline to much larger
Baghdad, Karachi and Nairobi networks. Their published maps use a common
visual language, but they should not be ranked by total route length or
fleet count alone. A larger mapped network may reflect a larger study area,
more radial branches or an assumed service level rather than stronger
evidence. Population context in a dossier is not a travel survey. A common
peak-flow brief is useful for testing a calculation but cannot substitute
for trips by origin, destination and time of day.

The most productive comparison asks what evidence would change a city
decision. For a compact network, it might be access to two closely spaced
stops. For a regional ring, it might be whether the proposed orbit serves
real cross-town demand or merely adds a long line and fleet requirement.
For a river crossing, it may be bridge location, flood level and land take.
For a hillside city, gradients and station access may govern. The map is
the beginning of that discussion, not its conclusion.

## From picture to project

A city can start by choosing one useful corridor, gathering observed demand
and walking routes, and marking the places where ground, land or utilities
could prevent construction. It can then compare several alignments under
the same passenger brief. Each revision should retain its own route,
stations, service, vehicle, civil assumptions and price basis. A changed
station can alter travel time and energy while leaving the train unchanged;
the resulting report should say both. The later engineering and delivery
work has to follow the selected revision, not an appealing figure borrowed
from another city.

The atlas can also help communicate uncertainty to people who do not read
engineering calculations. A public meeting can discuss why a proposed
line serves one district, what would be needed to cross a road safely, or
which destinations have been omitted. The map should be presented with
those questions and the missing evidence, so that feedback can alter the
proposal before a more expensive design is commissioned.
